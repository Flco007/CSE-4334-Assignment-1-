####################################################################################################
1. VALIDATION CURVES VS. LEARNING CURVES
####################################################################################################

MY QUESTION:

What is a validation curve in machine learning, and how is it different from a learning curve? What
do training and validation scores plotted against a hyperparameter tell you about underfitting and
overfitting?

CLAUDE'S RESPONSE:

A validation curve plots model performance against values of a single hyperparameter, with
everything else held fixed. A learning curve plots performance against training set size, with the
hyperparameters held fixed. Both show training and validation (cross-validated) scores side by side,
but they answer different questions.

The difference
--------------

                 | Validation curve                         | Learning curve
-----------------+------------------------------------------+---------------------------------------
X-axis           | A hyperparameter (e.g., max_depth, C,    | Number of training samples
                 | alpha, polynomial degree, k in kNN)      |
Question it      | "What setting of this hyperparameter     | "Would more data help, or is the model
answers          | gives the best bias–variance trade-off?" | itself the limitation?"
Typical use      | Tuning or sanity-checking one knob       | Deciding whether to collect more data
                 |                                          | or change model capacity
scikit-learn     | sklearn.model_selection.validation_curve | sklearn.model_selection.learning_curve
                 | (ValidationCurveDisplay)                 | (LearningCurveDisplay)

Reading a validation curve
--------------------------

Arrange the x-axis so model complexity increases from left to right. For max_depth or polynomial
degree, larger values mean more complexity. For regularization strength (alpha, or 1/C), larger
values mean less complexity, so the curve reads mirrored. The plot usually has three regions.

1. Underfitting (high bias), at the low-complexity end
- The training score is low.
- The validation score is also low and close to the training score.
- The model can't even fit the data it was trained on, so adding capacity helps.

2. The sweet spot
- The validation score peaks here. This is the hyperparameter value you want.
- The training score is fairly high, and the gap between the two curves is small to moderate.

3. Overfitting (high variance), at the high-complexity end
- The training score keeps rising toward perfect, for example 100% accuracy for an unlimited-depth
  tree.
- The validation score levels off and then drops.
- The gap between the curves grows. The model is memorizing noise in the training set rather than
  learning patterns that generalize.

In short, the signs to look for are these:
- Both scores low and close together: underfitting.
- Training score high, validation score much lower, with the gap widening: overfitting.
- Validation score at its maximum: the best setting for that hyperparameter.

Most validation-curve tools also plot ±1 standard deviation bands across CV folds. Wide bands on the
validation curve mean the score is unstable, which often happens in the overfitting region or with
small datasets. If two hyperparameter values have overlapping bands, prefer the simpler model.

How the two curves work together
--------------------------------

The validation curve tells you where the model is underfitting or overfitting as you vary
complexity. The learning curve then tells you what to do about it:
- Underfitting on the learning curve: both curves converge quickly to a low score. More data won't
  help, so increase capacity or add features.
- Overfitting on the learning curve: there's a large gap that narrows as samples are added. More
  data, or more regularization, will likely help.

A common workflow is to use a validation curve to pick a reasonable hyperparameter value, then draw
a learning curve at that value to decide whether collecting more data is worth it.

Caveats
-------

- A validation curve varies one hyperparameter at a time. When hyperparameters interact, such as C
  and gamma in an RBF SVM, use a grid or random search instead.
- Choosing a hyperparameter from CV scores makes those scores slightly optimistic. Report final
  performance on a held-out test set, or use nested CV.

References:
- scikit-learn User Guide §3.5, "Validation curves: plotting scores to evaluate models"
  (https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/learning_curve.html)
- Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman, The Elements of Statistical Learning, 2nd ed., §2.9 and Ch. 7
  (bias–variance trade-off and model assessment)
- Géron, Hands-On Machine Learning, 3rd ed., Ch. 4 (learning curves)

####################################################################################################
2. WHAT GRIDSEARCHCV.CV_RESULTS_ CONTAINS
####################################################################################################

MY QUESTION:

In scikit-learn, what does GridSearchCV.cv_results_ contain? Explain the keys like mean_test_score,
std_test_score, mean_train_score, rank_test_score, and the param_... columns, and how to turn it
into a pandas DataFrame.

CLAUDE'S RESPONSE:

cv_results_ is a dict of NumPy arrays. Each key is a column, and each array has one entry per
candidate, meaning one hyperparameter combination from the grid. It is shaped so that
pd.DataFrame(gs.cv_results_) gives you one row per candidate.

The keys
--------

Suppose param_grid = {"C": [0.1, 1, 10], "kernel": ["linear", "rbf"]} with cv=5. That gives 6
candidates, so every array has length 6.

Timing:
Key                             | Meaning
--------------------------------+-------------------------------------------------------------------
mean_fit_time, std_fit_time     | Seconds spent fitting the model, averaged over the CV folds, with
                                | the spread
mean_score_time, std_score_time | Seconds spent scoring on the validation fold

Parameters:
Key                   | Meaning
----------------------+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
param_C, param_kernel | One column per hyperparameter, named param_<name>. For pipelines it's
                      | param_<step>__<name>, e.g. param_svc__C.
params                | A list of dicts with the full parameter setting for each candidate, e.g.
                      | {'C': 1, 'kernel': 'rbf'}. This is the easiest one to pass back into
                      | set_params().

The param_... columns are NumPy masked arrays with dtype=object. When param_grid is a list of grids,
a parameter that doesn't apply to a candidate is masked. For example, gamma is masked for the
kernel='linear' grid. Once you build a DataFrame, those masked entries show up as NaN or --.

Test (validation-fold) scores:
Key                                   | Meaning
--------------------------------------+-------------------------------------------------------------
split0_test_score … split4_test_score | The score on each held-out fold
mean_test_score                       | The unweighted mean of the split scores. This is what the
                                      | search optimizes.
std_test_score                        | The standard deviation across folds, i.e. how stable the
                                      | candidate is
rank_test_score                       | 1 is best, ranked by mean_test_score. Tied candidates share
                                      | a rank. Candidates whose scores are NaN (failed fits) get
                                      | the worst rank.

Train scores (only if return_train_score=True):
Key                               | Meaning
----------------------------------+--------------------------------------------
split0_train_score …              | The score on each training portion
mean_train_score, std_train_score | Mean and standard deviation of those scores

return_train_score defaults to False because computing it costs extra time, so these keys are
missing unless you turn it on. They are worth having because the gap mean_train_score −
mean_test_score is the same overfitting signal you'd read from a validation curve. A high train
score combined with a much lower test score means the model is overfitting. Low scores on both mean
it is underfitting.

Multi-metric scoring:
If you pass scoring={"acc": "accuracy", "f1": "f1_macro"}, then:
- Every score key gets a metric suffix instead of _score: mean_test_acc, std_test_f1, rank_test_acc,
  split0_train_f1, and so on.
- refit must name the metric to pick the winner by, e.g. refit="f1". After that, best_index_,
  best_params_, and best_score_ refer to that metric.

Score sign convention:
Scores are always "higher is better." Error metrics are therefore negated. With
scoring="neg_mean_squared_error" you'll see values like -12.4, and the best candidate is the one
closest to zero. Flip the sign when you report the result.

Turning it into a DataFrame
---------------------------

    import pandas as pd
    from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV
    from sklearn.svm import SVC
    
    gs = GridSearchCV(
        SVC(),
        param_grid={"C": [0.1, 1, 10], "kernel": ["linear", "rbf"]},
        cv=5,
        return_train_score=True,   # needed for the train_* columns
    )
    gs.fit(X, y)
    
    df = pd.DataFrame(gs.cv_results_)
    
    # Keep the useful columns and sort by rank
    cols = ["params", "mean_train_score", "mean_test_score",
            "std_test_score", "rank_test_score"]
    summary = df[cols].sort_values("rank_test_score")
    
    # Add an overfitting gap column
    summary["gap"] = summary["mean_train_score"] - summary["mean_test_score"]
    print(summary.to_string(index=False))

Useful tricks:

Expand params into proper typed columns. The param_* columns hold object dtype, which can be awkward
to sort or plot:

    params_df = pd.json_normalize(df["params"])
    tidy = pd.concat([params_df, df[["mean_test_score", "std_test_score",
                                     "rank_test_score"]]], axis=1)

Pivot into a heatmap-style table for two hyperparameters:

    tidy.pivot(index="C", columns="kernel", values="mean_test_score")

Get the row of the selected model. This is equivalent to gs.best_params_ and gs.best_score_:

    df.loc[gs.best_index_, ["params", "mean_test_score"]]

Apply the one-standard-error rule. Instead of the absolute best candidate, choose the simplest model
whose mean_test_score is within one std_test_score of the best. GridSearchCV supports this through a
callable refit. The scikit-learn gallery has an example titled "Balance model complexity and cross-
validated score."

Pull per-fold scores for paired comparisons between candidates:

    fold_cols = df.filter(regex=r"^split\d+_test_score$")

Gotchas
-------
- cv_results_ only exists after .fit().
- std_test_score measures spread across folds. It is not a confidence interval, and the folds are
  not independent, so don't over-interpret small differences in mean_test_score.
- RandomizedSearchCV, HalvingGridSearchCV, and HalvingRandomSearchCV produce the same structure. The
  halving searches add iter and n_resources columns, so filter to the last iteration before ranking.

References:
- scikit-learn API: GridSearchCV (https://scikit-
  learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.model_selection.GridSearchCV.html) (attribute
  descriptions for cv_results_, best_index_, refit)
- scikit-learn User Guide §3.2, "Tuning the hyper-parameters of an estimator" (https://scikit-
  learn.org/stable/modules/grid_search.html) (multi-metric search and successive halving)
- Gallery: "Custom refit strategy of a grid search with cross-validation" (https://scikit-
  learn.org/stable/auto_examples/model_selection/plot_grid_search_digits.html)

####################################################################################################
3. MATPLOTLIB'S OBJECT-ORIENTED STYLE
####################################################################################################

MY QUESTION:

Teach me the matplotlib object-oriented style (fig, ax = plt.subplots()). Show a short example that
plots two lines, adds a shaded band with fill_between, draws a vertical marker line with axvline,
and labels the axes, title, and legend.

CLAUDE'S RESPONSE:

In the object-oriented (OO) style you create the figure and axes objects yourself and call methods
on them. The alternative, the pyplot style, calls plt.plot(...) and relies on hidden global state
about the "current" axes.

The two objects
---------------

- Figure: the whole canvas or window. You use it to save the image, set the overall size, add a
  super-title, and lay out subplots.
- Axes: one plot area, with its own x- and y-axis, data, labels, and legend. A figure can hold many
  of them. This is where nearly all plotting happens.

    fig, ax = plt.subplots()             # one Figure, one Axes
    fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2)       # one Figure, an array of 2 Axes

Why use it
----------

- It's explicit: each call names the Axes it changes. With multiple subplots, pyplot's "current
  axes" behavior quietly causes bugs.
- It scales cleanly to grids of subplots and to functions that take an ax argument.
- The matplotlib docs recommend the OO interface for anything beyond quick interactive plots.

pyplot vs OO method names
-------------------------

Most functions have the same name in both styles. The labeling functions gain a set_ prefix in OO
style:

pyplot        | OO (ax.)
--------------+----------------
plt.plot()    | ax.plot()
plt.xlabel()  | ax.set_xlabel()
plt.ylabel()  | ax.set_ylabel()
plt.title()   | ax.set_title()
plt.xlim()    | ax.set_xlim()
plt.legend()  | ax.legend()
plt.savefig() | fig.savefig()

Example: a validation curve
---------------------------

This example plots training and validation scores against a hyperparameter, shades ±1 standard
deviation with fill_between, and marks the best value with axvline.

    import numpy as np
    import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
    
    # --- Fake data shaped like a validation curve ---
    depth = np.arange(1, 16)
    train_mean = 1 - 0.5 * np.exp(-depth / 3)            # keeps rising
    val_mean   = 0.85 - 0.004 * (depth - 6) ** 2         # peaks, then falls
    val_std    = 0.01 + 0.004 * depth                     # gets noisier
    best_depth = depth[np.argmax(val_mean)]
    
    # --- OO plotting ---
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4.5))
    
    ax.plot(depth, train_mean, marker="o", label="Training score")
    ax.plot(depth, val_mean,   marker="s", label="Validation score")
    
    ax.fill_between(
        depth,
        val_mean - val_std,
        val_mean + val_std,
        alpha=0.2,                 # transparency so the line stays visible
        label="Validation ±1 std",
    )
    
    ax.axvline(best_depth, color="gray", linestyle="--",
               label=f"Best max_depth = {best_depth}")
    
    ax.set_xlabel("max_depth")
    ax.set_ylabel("Accuracy")
    ax.set_title("Validation curve for a decision tree")
    ax.legend(loc="lower right")
    ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
    
    fig.tight_layout()             # stops labels from getting clipped
    fig.savefig("validation_curve.png", dpi=150)
    plt.show()

What each call does:

- ax.plot(x, y, label=...): draws a line. Each call adds a new line, and the label is what appears
  in the legend.
- ax.fill_between(x, y_lower, y_upper, alpha=...): shades the region between two curves. It's the
  standard way to show variance bands. Use color= to match the band to its line, for example by
  grabbing the color with line, = ax.plot(...) and then passing color=line.get_color().
- ax.axvline(x, ...): draws a vertical line that spans the full height of the axes, whatever the
  y-range. Its counterpart is ax.axhline(y). To span only a range, use ax.vlines(x, ymin, ymax).
- ax.legend(): collects every artist that has a label. You can pass loc="best" or a specific
  location.
- fig.tight_layout(): adjusts spacing so labels and titles fit. Another option is plt.subplots(...,
  layout="constrained"), which is often better with multiple subplots.

Scaling to multiple subplots
----------------------------

Here the OO style really pays off, because each Axes is its own object:

    fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4), sharey=True,
                                   layout="constrained")
    
    ax1.plot(depth, train_mean, label="Train")
    ax1.plot(depth, val_mean, label="Val")
    ax1.set_title("Validation curve")
    ax1.set_xlabel("max_depth")
    ax1.set_ylabel("Accuracy")
    ax1.legend()
    
    ax2.plot(np.linspace(50, 1000, 15), val_mean, label="Val")
    ax2.set_title("Learning curve")
    ax2.set_xlabel("Training samples")
    ax2.legend()
    
    fig.suptitle("Model diagnostics")

With a grid like plt.subplots(2, 3), axes is a 2-D array. Index it as axes[0, 1], or loop with for
ax in axes.flat:.

A reusable pattern
------------------

Write plotting helpers that accept an ax. That way they work inside any layout:

    def plot_curve(ax, x, mean, std, label):
        line, = ax.plot(x, mean, label=label)
        ax.fill_between(x, mean - std, mean + std,
                        color=line.get_color(), alpha=0.2)
        return line
    
    fig, ax = plt.subplots()
    plot_curve(ax, depth, val_mean, val_std, "Validation")

Seaborn and scikit-learn's *Display classes follow the same convention, for example
ValidationCurveDisplay.from_estimator(..., ax=ax).

4. GRIDSEARCHCV ON A DECISIONTREECLASSIFIER, WITH A PLOTTED VALIDATION CURVE
####################################################################################################

MY QUESTION:

Write a small example that runs GridSearchCV on a DecisionTreeClassifier with a toy dataset, tuning
max_depth and criterion. Then plot mean test score vs. max_depth with ±1 standard deviation bands,
one line per criterion, with training scores shown as dashed lines. Explain each step.

CLAUDE'S RESPONSE:

The script (tree_gridsearch_curve.py)
-------------------------------------

    """GridSearchCV on a DecisionTreeClassifier, tuning max_depth and criterion,
    then plotting a validation curve per criterion from cv_results_."""
    
    import numpy as np
    import pandas as pd
    import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
    from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
    from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV, StratifiedKFold, train_test_split
    from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
    
    # 1. Data: hold out a test set the search never sees
    X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
    X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
        X, y, test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=42
    )
    
    # 2. Search space
    param_grid = {
        "max_depth": list(range(1, 16)),
        "criterion": ["gini", "entropy"],
    }
    
    # 3. Cross-validation scheme (shuffled, stratified, reproducible)
    cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=42)
    
    # 4. Grid search
    search = GridSearchCV(
        estimator=DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=42),
        param_grid=param_grid,
        scoring="accuracy",
        cv=cv,
        return_train_score=True,  # needed for the dashed training lines
        n_jobs=-1,
    )
    search.fit(X_train, y_train)
    
    print(f"Best params:      {search.best_params_}")
    print(f"Best CV accuracy: {search.best_score_:.4f}")
    print(f"Test accuracy:    {search.score(X_test, y_test):.4f}")
    
    # 5. cv_results_ -> tidy DataFrame
    results = pd.DataFrame(search.cv_results_)
    results = pd.concat(
        [
            pd.json_normalize(results["params"]),  # typed max_depth / criterion
            results[["mean_test_score", "std_test_score",
                     "mean_train_score", "std_train_score", "rank_test_score"]],
        ],
        axis=1,
    )
    print("\nTop 5 candidates:")
    print(results.sort_values("rank_test_score").head().to_string(index=False))
    
    # 6. Plot: one colour per criterion, solid = validation, dashed = training
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 5), layout="constrained")
    
    for criterion, grp in results.groupby("criterion"):
        grp = grp.sort_values("max_depth")
        depth = grp["max_depth"].to_numpy()
        test_mean = grp["mean_test_score"].to_numpy()
        test_std = grp["std_test_score"].to_numpy()
    
        line, = ax.plot(depth, test_mean, marker="o",
                        label=f"{criterion} - validation")
        color = line.get_color()
        ax.fill_between(depth, test_mean - test_std, test_mean + test_std,
                        color=color, alpha=0.15)
        ax.plot(depth, grp["mean_train_score"], linestyle="--", color=color,
                label=f"{criterion} - training")
    
    best_depth = search.best_params_["max_depth"]
    ax.axvline(best_depth, color="gray", linestyle=":",
               label=f"Best: depth={best_depth}, {search.best_params_['criterion']}")
    
    ax.set_xlabel("max_depth")
    ax.set_ylabel("Accuracy")
    ax.set_title("Decision tree validation curve (5-fold CV, ±1 std)")
    ax.set_xticks(param_grid["max_depth"])
    ax.legend(loc="lower right")
    ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
    
    fig.savefig("tree_validation_curve.png", dpi=150)
    plt.show()

The search picked criterion='entropy', max_depth=3, which scored 0.939 CV accuracy and 0.951 on the
held-out test set.

Step by step
------------

1. Load data and hold out a test set

    X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
    X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
        X, y, test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=42)

The breast cancer dataset has 569 samples, 30 features, and a binary label. The test set is split
off before any tuning, so search.score(X_test, y_test) gives an honest estimate. The CV scores
themselves are slightly optimistic, because the hyperparameter was chosen to maximize them.
stratify=y keeps the class ratio the same in both splits.

2. Define the grid

    param_grid = {"max_depth": list(range(1, 16)), "criterion": ["gini", "entropy"]}

This gives 15 × 2 = 30 candidates. The keys must match the estimator's constructor arguments
exactly.

3. Choose the CV splitter

    cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=42)

Passing cv=5 alone would also stratify for a classifier, but it wouldn't shuffle. Defining the
splitter explicitly makes the folds shuffled and reproducible.

4. Run the search

    search = GridSearchCV(DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=42), param_grid,
                          scoring="accuracy", cv=cv, return_train_score=True, n_jobs=-1)
    search.fit(X_train, y_train)

- random_state=42 on the tree matters because trees break ties between equally good splits randomly.
  Without it, scores shift a little from run to run.
- return_train_score=True is required for the dashed training lines. It defaults to False.
- n_jobs=-1 runs the 30 × 5 = 150 fits in parallel.
- By default, refit=True retrains the best candidate on all of X_train, which is the model
  search.score() uses.

5. Tidy cv_results_

    results = pd.DataFrame(search.cv_results_)
    results = pd.concat([pd.json_normalize(results["params"]), results[[...score cols...]]], axis=1)

pd.json_normalize(results["params"]) turns the list of parameter dicts into proper max_depth (int)
and criterion (str) columns. This avoids the object-dtype masked param_* columns, so sorting and
grouping behave.

6. Plot one curve per criterion

    for criterion, grp in results.groupby("criterion"):
        grp = grp.sort_values("max_depth")
        line, = ax.plot(depth, test_mean, marker="o", label=f"{criterion} - validation")
        color = line.get_color()
        ax.fill_between(depth, test_mean - test_std, test_mean + test_std, color=color, alpha=0.15)
        ax.plot(depth, grp["mean_train_score"], linestyle="--", color=color, label=...)

- groupby("criterion") splits the table into one sub-table per criterion.
- sort_values("max_depth") puts the points in order so the lines don't zig-zag.
- Taking line.get_color() from the validation line and reusing it gives each criterion one color.
  The solid line is validation, the dashed line is training, and the band is ±1 std across folds.
- axvline marks search.best_params_["max_depth"].

Reading the plot
----------------

- Depth 1–2, underfitting: both training and validation accuracy are relatively low. A single split,
  or two, can't capture the structure.
- Depth 3, the sweet spot: validation accuracy peaks at about 0.939, and training accuracy is about
  0.975. The gap is small.
- Depth 4 and up, overfitting: training accuracy climbs to exactly 1.0 by about depth 7–8, meaning
  the tree memorizes the training folds. Validation drops slightly and then flattens around
  0.925–0.93. It flattens because once every leaf is pure, a larger max_depth does nothing; the tree
  simply stops growing.
- Gini vs. entropy: at depth 3 the two criteria have the same mean score to 6 decimals (0.938960).
  Entropy is ranked first only by a floating-point hair. The ±1 std bands overlap almost everywhere,
  so the criterion doesn't meaningfully matter here; depth does. If anything, entropy's lower fold-
  to-fold std at depth 3 (0.014 vs. 0.022) makes it the slightly more stable pick.

The ranking also shows the tie handling discussed earlier. Entropy at depths 7 and 11 both get
rank_test_score = 4, because once the tree is fully grown they produce identical models.

A natural next step is to add min_samples_leaf or ccp_alpha (cost-complexity pruning) to the grid.
These regularize the tree more smoothly than capping depth alone.

5. PIPELINES, __ PARAMETER NAMES, AND FILTERING DATAFRAMES
####################################################################################################

MY QUESTION:

In scikit-learn, when a model is inside a Pipeline and tuned with GridSearchCV, why do parameter
names use a double underscore like clf__max_depth, and how does that change the column names in
cv_results_? Also show how to filter a pandas DataFrame to rows matching two conditions, then sort
by one column.

CLAUDE'S RESPONSE:

Why the double underscore
-------------------------

A Pipeline is itself an estimator, and its hyperparameters include the hyperparameters of every step
inside it. scikit-learn exposes them all through one flat namespace using the pattern
<step_name>__<param_name>:

    from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
    from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
    from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
    
    pipe = Pipeline([
        ("scaler", StandardScaler()),
        ("clf", DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=42)),
    ])
    
    pipe.get_params().keys()
    # ... 'scaler__with_mean', 'scaler__with_std', 'clf__max_depth', 'clf__criterion', ...
    
    pipe.set_params(clf__max_depth=3)   # reaches into the "clf" step and sets max_depth

GridSearchCV works by calling set_params(**candidate) on a clone of the estimator for each
candidate. Your param_grid keys therefore have to be names that the pipeline's set_params
understands. A bare max_depth raises ValueError: Invalid parameter 'max_depth' for estimator
Pipeline(...) because the pipeline itself has no max_depth.

The separator is a double underscore because single underscores already appear in ordinary parameter
names (max_depth, min_samples_leaf). If the separator were _, clf_max_depth would be ambiguous. With
__, splitting on the separator is unambiguous.

The convention nests as deep as your estimators do:

Structure                                                        | Parameter name
-----------------------------------------------------------------+----------------------------------
Pipeline step                                                    | clf__max_depth
ColumnTransformer inside a pipeline                              | prep__num__with_mean
Pipeline inside a ColumnTransformer inside a pipeline            | prep__num__scaler__with_mean
Meta-estimator such as BaggingClassifier(estimator=Tree())       | clf__estimator__max_depth
make_pipeline(...), which names steps after the lowercased class | decisiontreeclassifier__max_depth

You can also tune which estimator occupies a step, or skip a step entirely:

    param_grid = [
        {"scaler": [StandardScaler(), "passthrough"],   # try with and without scaling
         "clf": [DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=42)],
         "clf__max_depth": [2, 3, 4]},
        {"clf": [LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)],
         "clf__C": [0.1, 1, 10]},
    ]

When unsure of a name, print sorted(pipe.get_params()) and copy from there.

How it changes cv_results_
--------------------------

Only the parameter-related entries change. Score and timing keys keep the same names.

Without a pipeline                            | With a pipeline (step named clf)
----------------------------------------------+-----------------------------------------------------
param_max_depth                               | param_clf__max_depth
param_criterion                               | param_clf__criterion
params: {'max_depth': 3, 'criterion': 'gini'} | params: {'clf__max_depth': 3, 'clf__criterion':
                                              | 'gini'}
best_params_: {'max_depth': 3, ...}           | best_params_: {'clf__max_depth': 3, ...}
mean_test_score, rank_test_score, …           | unchanged

If you used pd.json_normalize(df["params"]), the expanded columns become clf__max_depth and
clf__criterion. To drop the prefix for plotting or reporting:

    results.columns = results.columns.str.replace(r"^clf__", "", regex=True)

Filtering on two conditions, then sorting
-----------------------------------------

Here is the earlier example, wrapped in a pipeline:

    import pandas as pd
    from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
    from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV, StratifiedKFold
    
    X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
    
    search = GridSearchCV(
        pipe,
        param_grid={"clf__max_depth": list(range(1, 16)),
                    "clf__criterion": ["gini", "entropy"]},
        cv=StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=42),
        return_train_score=True,
    ).fit(X, y)
    
    raw = pd.DataFrame(search.cv_results_)
    results = pd.concat(
        [pd.json_normalize(raw["params"]),          # clf__max_depth (int), clf__criterion (str)
         raw[["mean_test_score", "std_test_score", "mean_train_score", "rank_test_score"]]],
        axis=1,
    )

Option 1: boolean masks (the most common form):

    mask = (results["clf__criterion"] == "gini") & (results["clf__max_depth"] <= 5)
    subset = results.loc[mask].sort_values("mean_test_score", ascending=False)

- Use & for AND, | for OR, and ~ for NOT. Python's and/or raise ValueError: The truth value of a
  Series is ambiguous.
- Wrap each condition in parentheses. & binds more tightly than == and <=, so leaving them out
  produces a wrong comparison or an error.
- .loc[mask] is the explicit form. results[mask] also works.

Option 2: .query() (often more readable):

    subset = (results
              .query("clf__criterion == 'gini' and clf__max_depth <= 5")
              .sort_values("mean_test_score", ascending=False))

Inside a query string you can use and/or. Names with __ are valid identifiers, so they work as
written. For a column name containing spaces or dots, wrap it in backticks: `mean test` > 0.9. Use @
to reference a Python variable: .query("clf__max_depth <= @max_d").

Useful variations:

    # Membership and ranges
    results[results["clf__max_depth"].isin([3, 5, 7]) & results["clf__criterion"].eq("entropy")]
    results[results["clf__max_depth"].between(3, 6)]          # inclusive on both ends
    
    # Sort by several columns: best score first, then the shallower tree to break ties
    results.sort_values(["mean_test_score", "clf__max_depth"], ascending=[False, True])
    
    # Filter, sort, take the top 3, and keep only some columns
    (results.loc[mask]
            .sort_values("mean_test_score", ascending=False)
            .head(3)[["clf__max_depth", "mean_test_score", "std_test_score"]])

A gotcha with the raw param_* columns:

If you filter directly on pd.DataFrame(search.cv_results_) without using json_normalize, the
param_clf__max_depth column has object dtype, and with a list of grids it can contain masked values.
Equality checks like == "gini" still work. Numeric comparisons and sorting are more reliable after
converting:

    raw["param_clf__max_depth"] = pd.to_numeric(raw["param_clf__max_depth"], errors="coerce")

This is why the json_normalize(params) approach is usually cleaner: it gives you properly typed
columns from the start.

- scikit-learn User Guide §3.2.3, "Composite estimators and parameter spaces" (https://scikit-

6. AX.ANNOTATE AND AX.SET_XTICKS
####################################################################################################

MY QUESTION:

In matplotlib, how do I use ax.annotate to label a point on a plot, and ax.set_xticks to force
whole-number ticks on the x-axis?

CLAUDE'S RESPONSE:

ax.annotate: labeling a point
-----------------------------

    ax.annotate(text, xy=(x, y), xytext=(dx, dy), textcoords="offset points",
                arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->"))

Argument                   | What it does
---------------------------+------------------------------------------------------------------------
text                       | The label string
xy                         | The point being labeled, in data coordinates by default
                           | (xycoords="data")
xytext                     | Where the text goes. Without textcoords, this is also in data
                           | coordinates.
textcoords="offset points" | Makes xytext a pixel-like offset from xy, e.g. (10, -20) means 10 pt
                           | right and 20 pt down. This is usually the best choice, because the
                           | label stays in place when the axis limits change.
arrowprops                 | A dict that draws an arrow from the text to xy. Leave it out to place
                           | text with no arrow.
ha, va                     | Horizontal and vertical alignment of the text relative to xytext
bbox                       | A box behind the text, e.g. dict(boxstyle="round", fc="white",
                           | alpha=0.8)

ax.set_xticks: whole-number ticks
---------------------------------

There are three ways to do it.

1. Explicit ticks. Use this when you know the values, such as a hyperparameter grid:

    ax.set_xticks(range(1, 16))                     # a tick at every integer from 1 to 15
    ax.set_xticks([1, 3, 5], labels=["one", "three", "five"])   # custom labels (matplotlib ≥ 3.5)

2. A locator that only chooses integers. It still thins ticks automatically when the range is large,
so it's the best general-purpose option:

    from matplotlib.ticker import MaxNLocator
    ax.xaxis.set_major_locator(MaxNLocator(integer=True))

3. A tick every N units:

    from matplotlib.ticker import MultipleLocator
    ax.xaxis.set_major_locator(MultipleLocator(1))   # every 1; use 2, 5, etc. for sparser ticks

set_xticks fixes the tick positions permanently. If you later zoom or change xlim, ticks outside the
list disappear and no new ones are added. The locator approaches recompute the ticks whenever the
view changes.

Example
-------

This continues the decision-tree validation curve. It labels the best point and forces integer depth
ticks:

    import numpy as np
    import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
    from matplotlib.ticker import MaxNLocator
    
    depth = np.arange(1, 16)
    val_mean = 0.94 - 0.004 * (depth - 3) ** 2
    val_mean = np.maximum(val_mean, 0.925)          # flattens out, like the real curve
    
    best_i = np.argmax(val_mean)
    best_x, best_y = depth[best_i], val_mean[best_i]
    
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4), layout="constrained")
    ax.plot(depth, val_mean, marker="o", label="Validation accuracy")
    
    # Label the best point: the text sits 30 pt right and 25 pt below it, with an arrow
    ax.annotate(
        f"best: depth={best_x}\nacc={best_y:.3f}",
        xy=(best_x, best_y),
        xytext=(30, -25),
        textcoords="offset points",
        ha="left", va="top",
        arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->", color="gray"),
        bbox=dict(boxstyle="round,pad=0.3", fc="white", ec="gray", alpha=0.9),
    )
    
    # Whole-number x ticks
    ax.set_xticks(depth)                             # every depth value from 1 to 15
    # or: ax.xaxis.set_major_locator(MaxNLocator(integer=True))
    
    ax.set_xlabel("max_depth")
    ax.set_ylabel("Accuracy")
    ax.legend(loc="lower right")
    plt.show()

Tips
----

- Labeling several points works in a loop. Small offsets without arrows look cleaner:

      for x, y in zip(depth, val_mean):
          ax.annotate(f"{y:.3f}", (x, y), xytext=(0, 6), textcoords="offset points",
                      ha="center", fontsize=8)

  For bar charts, ax.bar_label(bars, fmt="%.2f") does this in one call.
- Text near the edge getting clipped: move it toward the inside with a negative offset, or use
  layout="constrained" so the figure makes room. annotation_clip=False lets an annotation draw even
  when its xy point is outside the axes.
- Fixed positions in the frame: xycoords="axes fraction" places the label relative to the axes
  rather than the data. For example, xy=(0.02, 0.95) puts it in the top-left corner and keeps it
  there regardless of the data.
- Removing ticks: ax.set_xticks([]). Rotating labels: ax.tick_params(axis="x", labelrotation=45).

  (https://matplotlib.org/stable/users/explain/text/annotations.html), which covers coordinate
  systems, arrow styles, and offsets
- Axes.set_xticks API reference
  (https://matplotlib.org/stable/api/_as_gen/matplotlib.axes.Axes.set_xticks.html)
